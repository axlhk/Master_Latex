\chapter{Introduction}
In 1937 during the founding period of quantum field theory, Ettore Majorana hypothesised the existence of fermions which are their own antiparticle \cite{majoranaTeoriaSimmetricaDellelettrone1937}. These became known as Majorana fermions, and were for a long time considered to only be a property of very specific elementary particles, although the existence of such elementary particles is yet to be confirmed. The interest in Majorana fermions in condensed matter physics as quasi particles is a recent phenomenon, bolstered by the surge of interest in topological phases of matter. Originally this interest started in 1980 with the experimental confirmation of the quantum Hall effect \cite{klitzingNewMethodHighAccuracy1980}, which was later theorised to occur due to an arising topological invariant - a Chern number \cite{thoulessQuantizedHallConductance1982}. From this, interest became more widespread with physicists exploring materials characterised by topological phases, like topological insulators \cite{hasanColloquiumTopologicalInsulators2010}. In 2001 Kitaev proposed the Kitaev chain \cite{kitaevUnpairedMajoranaFermions2001a}. A one-dimensional superconducting wire with a topological phase which can host Majorana zero modes at the ends of the chain in the form of quasiparticles. Recent experiments have yielded signatures consistent with Majorana zero modes, although there is still strong debate on this topic as to whether or not they were measured with certainty \cite{frolovDataSharingHelps2026}. 

The Kitaev chain has garnered a lot of attention due to its promise of a topological qubit. As the MZMs are located at the edges of the chain, the overlap of their wavefunctions decreases exponentially with the length of the chain, and their stored information undergoes a topological protection in which decoherence is significantly reduced \cite{nayakNonAbelianAnyonsTopological2008}. In other words a solid theoretical understanding and a following experimental realization of the Kitaev chain could mean opening the door to a decoherence free quantum computer. Such a quantum computer could provide great acceleration in many fields where quantum algorithms such as the famed Grovers\cite{groverFastQuantumMechanical1996} or Shor's\cite{shorPolynomialTimeAlgorithmsPrime} are applicable, but can also offer exponential speedups in fields in which simulations of many-body quantum Hamiltonians are necessary \cite{feynmanSimulatingPhysicsComputers1982}. A specific example being high-temperature superconductivity \cite{nayakNonAbelianAnyonsTopological2008}, with many expected potential applications in broader fields such as medicine, condensed matter, materials science, chemistry, and more. 

There are however several issues the field must address for this to become reality. Among others we need a clear scalable method of implementation, measuring and braiding the Majoranas. We need to be able to understand the system at larger system sizes (where we expect the topological protection to be strong) which is currently unfeasible numerically due to the numerical cost increasing exponentially with the Hilbert space. We need a system which is tolerant enough against decoherence to perform about $10^6$ operations without errors in order to have sufficient error correction \cite{aharonovFaultTolerantQuantumComputation1999}. These are only examples of the theoretical problems that require attention. The engineering challenges of maintaining a superconductor, preventing quasiparticle poisoning, varying the parameters of the chain to move the MZMs, and more, represent their own challenges that will require marvels of engineering.

This thesis aims to create a solid understanding of the current state of the field, and open doors to further research. First we review the Kitaev chain; study its mathematical and conceptual origins, and then further explore how this system can be used to create a simple topological qubit. Further we will explore the systems theoretical applications, limitations, and properties with regards to a specific architecture, the T-junction. Designed to allow a scalable system of braidable Majoranas. Specifically we will study which operations are available to us in the form of braiding with non-abelian statistics. In continuation of this we will explore a way of numerically simulating the system with hopes of maintaining accuracy and reducing the numerical cost by considering an adiabatic approach and truncating higher energy states. For the results we will also discuss whether or not the braiding operations earlier described are in fact available to us by checking the degeneracy of the ground states throughout the simulation.
