\chapter{The Kitaev chain}
The Kitaev chain is a toy model of a one dimensional spinless p-wave superconductor, and is an example of material that can realize a topological superconducting phase. It consists of a chain of spinless fermions with nearest-neighbour hopping and superconducting pairing, under the influence of a chemical potential. The Hamiltonian takes the form 
\begin{align}
    H =& \sum_j\left[ - \omega \left(a_j^\dagger a_{j+1} + a_{j+1}^\dagger a_j  \right) - \mu \left(a_j^\dagger  a_j - \frac{1}{2} \right) + \Delta a_j a_{j+1} + \Delta^* a_{j+1}^\dagger a_j^\dagger \right]
    \label{eq:Original_Kitaev_Hamiltonian}
\end{align}
where $a_j$ and $a_j^\dagger$ are annihilation and creation operators for the fermionic sites $j = 1, 2, \cdots, L$ in the chain, where $L$ (also denoted $N$ for the T-junction later) is the total amount of sites. For the $\omega, \Delta$ terms we count the bonds, ranging $j = 1, 2, \cdots, L-1$. The parameter $\omega$ is the hopping amplitude, $\mu$ is the chemical potential, and $\Delta = |\Delta|e^{i\theta}$ is the induced superconducting gap. All of which can be made time dependent. The Hamiltonian serves to give rise to Majorana zero modes (MZMs) at its boundaries. Simply put, these MZMs are theorized to be useful as qubits, and a network of chains can be used for quantum computation. 

MZMs are interesting candidates for qubits as their topological nature provides robustness against local perturbations and certain sources of decoherence. Their non-Abelian nature allows for braiding, where the MZMs exchange position and information about the path is stored in the wavefunction. This is the core of the process that allows computation to take place, and we will study this closer in a later chapter \ref{chap:Quantum_computation}. 

In the first two sections \ref{sec:Majorana_Fermions}, \ref{sec:Phases_Kitaev_chain} of this chapter (and this heading) we closely follow the original theory laid out by Kitaev  \cite{kitaevUnpairedMajoranaFermions2001a}, re-deriving the main results and filling in with intermediate steps and discussion for clarity, with an addition of supplementary subsections. More specifically we cover the origin of the MZMs and the phases of the Kitaev chain. We also study BdG formalism and apply it to the Kitaev chain as parts of the phase discussion is based on these results. 

\section{Majorana fermions in the Kitaev chain} \label{sec:Majorana_Fermions}
In the context of the Kitaev chain, Majorana fermions are quasiparticles which are `half' of an ordinary fermion and also act as their own antiparticle. The Majorana operators can be defined as
\begin{align}
    c_{2j-1} = a_j + a_j^\dagger, \hspace{4mm}  c_{2j} = \frac{a_j - a_j^\dagger}{i}, \hspace{4mm} (j = 1, ..., N) \label{eq:majorana_op2j}
\end{align}
which are hermitian and anticommute, meaning they satisfy the relations
\begin{align}
    c_m^\dagger = c_m, \hspace{4mm} \{c_l, c_m\} = c_l c_m + c_m c_l = 2 \delta_{lm}, \hspace{4mm} (l,m = 1, ..., 2N). \label{eq:Majorana_Herm_anticomm}
\end{align}
Using the definitions of the majorana operators \ref{eq:majorana_op2j} we can find the number operator for a site $j$
\begin{align}
    a_j^\dagger a_j = \frac{1}{2} \left(1 + i c_{2j-1} c_{2j} \right),
\end{align}
where the term $i c_{2j-1} c_{2j}$ corresponds to a phase error. If $c_{2j-1}$ and $c_{2j}$ are not on the same site, but separated in space, then $c_{2j-1} c_{2j}$ would represent an interaction between two separated Majorana sites, requiring the environment to couple the Majorana fermions simultaneously to create the phase error. Physical noise and imperfections are typically local, so placing the Majorana sites far enough away from each other would decrease the risk of a phase error. Further, a single Majorana operator cannot appear by itself in a reasonable Hamiltonian as it would not preserve fermionic parity; flipping the system from an even to odd (or vice versa) number of fermions. As such, the Majorana operators will always come in pairs $c_i c_l$, and thus no parity preserving term can involve just one isolated Majorana. Together this tells us that errors would either have to break parity, or couple both separated Majoranas at once, which is highly unlikely for separated Majorana sites and thus the Majorana edge states are protected from local errors.

\subsection{Rewriting the Hamiltonian in terms of Majorana operators}
We can express the Hamiltonian in terms of Majorana operators. As we will see, this transformation will provide insight about the structure of the phases and the emergence of the edge modes. Before doing so we simplify the calculation by hiding the phase $\theta$ in the majorana operators
\begin{align}
    c_{2j-1} &= e^{i \frac{\theta}{2}} a_j + e^{-i \frac{\theta}{2}}a_j^\dagger \label{eq:c2j-1_hide_phase}\\
    c_{2j} &= -ie^{i \frac{\theta}{2}} a_j + ie^{-i \frac{\theta}{2}}a_j^\dagger \label{eq:c2j_hide_phase}.
\end{align}
We can now perform the transformation of the Hamiltonian by inverting these relations and inserting into the original Hamiltonian \ref{eq:Original_Kitaev_Hamiltonian}. Together with the hermiticity and anticommutation relations \ref{eq:Majorana_Herm_anticomm} we get the Hamiltonian
\begin{align}
    H &= \frac{i}{2} \sum_j \left[-\mu c_{2j-1} c_{2j} + \left(\omega + \left|\Delta \right|  \right) c_{2j} c_{2j+1} + \left(-\omega + \left| \Delta \right| \right) c_{2j-1} c_{2j+2} \right].
\end{align}
This calculation is shown in appendix \ref{Appendix:Hamiltonian_Transformation_Majorana_ops}. Staring at this Hamiltonian, we can first see that it is on the general quadratic Majorana form
\begin{align}
    H = \frac{i}{4} \sum_{l, m} A_{lm} c_{l} c_m, \hspace{4mm} A^T = -A\label{eq:quadratic_H}
\end{align}
with A being a real and antisymmetric matrix, encoding the intrinsic particle-hole symmetry of the superconducting system. The single particle spectrum is fully determined by the eigenvalues of $A$, reducing the problem from a $2^{L}$-dimensional many-body operator problem in Fock space to a single-particle linear algebra problem on a $2L \times 2L$ matrix. This is the basis of BdG, as we will get back to later. Further, we see that all direct mentions of the complex phase $\theta$ are gone from $H$. This reflects that $\theta$ can be removed by an appropriate gauge transformation, and is therefore irrelevant for the bulk spectrum and the topological classification of the phases, which we will also touch upon later.

The Majorana representation also gives us a geometric picture of the chain. The Hamiltonian now consists of three terms, providing three different types of couplings. An on-site term $-\mu c_{2j-1} c_{2j}$, and two nearest-neighbour terms $(\omega + |\Delta|)c_{2j} c_{2j+1}$ and $(-\omega + |\Delta|) c_{2j-1} c_{2j+2}$. The on-site term couples two Majorana modes per site $j$, so for a chain of length $L$ we get $2L$ Majorana operators. We can garner further insight by studying specific cases for the parameters $\mu, \omega$ and $|\Delta|$ of the Hamiltonian.

\subsubsection{Topologically trivial case \texorpdfstring{$\boldsymbol{\omega = |\Delta| = 0, \mu > 0}$} {$\omega = |\Delta| = 0, \mu > 0$}}
In the case where $\mu$ dominates the Hamiltonian reduces to
\begin{align}
    H &= -\mu \sum_j \left(a_j^\dagger a_j - \frac{1}{2} \right) = \frac{i}{2} \mu \sum_j c_{2j-1}c_{2j}.
\end{align}
In this case the system is topologically trivial as the Majoranas on each site are glued together to form a complex fermion. There are no unpaired Majorana fermions at the edges of the chain, making this case uninteresting for further study.

\subsubsection{Topologically non-trivial case $\boldsymbol{\omega} = \boldsymbol{|\Delta|} > 0, \boldsymbol{\mu} = 0$}
In the case where the hopping and superconducting parameters dominate the Hamiltonian reduces to
\begin{align}
  H = i\sum_{j} \omega c_{2j} c_{2j+1}, \label{eq:H_tdelta_mu0}  
\end{align}
where the nearest neighbours between different sites dominate. This is an interesting case as $c_2$ couples to $c_3$, $c_4$ couples to $c_5$, and so on, meaning the majorana operators from different sites pair together. But $c_1$ and $c_{2L}$, the Majorana edge states, do not appear in the Hamiltonian. They have however not disappeared physically, but rather remain as zero-energy Majorana modes localized at the edges of the wire. Together they form a nonlocal fermionic zero mode. This is the most important insight from the transformation of the Hamiltonian as these are the states we were looking to create.

The two cases above can be thought of by a common intuitive picture of a chain of people with two hands. The people represent the sites, and their hands represent the two majorana fermions associated with the site. In the trivial case everyone is holding their own hands, representing how the majorana fermions have paired together at the same site. Whereas in the topologically non-trivial case the people have let go of their own hands, and are now holding hands with their neighbours, representing how the majorana fermions are now paired with the majorana of the neighboring site. Now there are suddenly two open hands: one at each end, representing the two topologically separated majoranas.

We can now define new annihilation and creation operators spanning the sites $j$ and $j+1$
\begin{align}
    d_j &= \frac{1}{2} \left(c_{2j} + i c_{2j+1} \right) \\
    d_j^\dagger &= \frac{1}{2} \left(c_{2j} - i c_{2j+1} \right). \label{eq:define_dj}
\end{align}
Such that we can rewrite the Hamiltonian 
\begin{align}
    H &= 2 \omega \sum_{j = 1} \left(f_j^\dagger f_j - \frac{1}{2} \right).
\end{align}
As the edge states are not mentioned in the Hamiltonian, this expression fully determines the energy spectrum of the bulk. We see that the bulk consists of fermionic modes with energy $\propto 2\omega$. Further, the ground states satisfy the condition $d_j |\psi \rangle = 0 $ for all $j$. As such, all these modes will remain empty, and any ground state $|\psi \rangle $ is fully determined by the edge majoranas $c_1$ and $c_{2L}$. Therefore we get two orthogonal states where the above ground state condition for the bulk is true; $|\psi_0\rangle$ and $|\psi_1 \rangle$. These ground states will be eigenstates of the hermitian operator $-i c_{1} c_{2L}$ as the edge Majoranas represent unpaired degrees of freedom. This operator also squares to 1, and as such has eigenvalues $\pm 1$. We get 
\begin{align}
    - i c_{1} c_{2L} |\psi_0 \rangle = |\psi_0\rangle, \hspace{4mm} - i c_{1} c_{2L} |\psi_1 \rangle = -|\psi_1\rangle,
\end{align}
where the labeling of the states were chosen arbitrarily. We see that the total fermion parity operator
\begin{align}
    P = \prod_j \left(- i c_{2j-1} c_{2j} \right)
\end{align}
has the same contribution from the bulk for both ground states. So the difference in parity is solely a result of the edge contribution $-i c_1 c_{2L}$. The even parity of $|\psi_0 \rangle$ represents the state being a superposition of states with an even number of electrons. And $|\psi_1 \rangle$ has an odd parity, representing an odd number of electrons. Together they reflect the boundary degrees of freedom from the unpaired Majorana modes at the edges. 

\subsubsection{Arbitrary values of $\boldsymbol{\omega}$, $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, $\boldsymbol{\Delta}$}
For arbitrary values of $\omega$, $\mu$, $\Delta$ the Hamiltonian has the general quadratic form (\ref{eq:quadratic_H}) in terms of the Majorana operators. This means the Hamiltonian can be diagonalized into independent fermionic modes by a change of basis, implemented by a matrix $W$. We require that $W$ is a $2N \times 2N$ real orthogonal $(W^T W = W W^T = I)$ matrix, and that it has two further properties; the first being that it defines new operators $\{b_m', b_m''\} $ by the linear transformation $b = Wc$, or
\begin{align}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        b_1'\\
        b_1''\\
        \vdots\\
        b_N'\\
        b_N''
    \end{pmatrix} = W \begin{pmatrix}
        c_1 \\
        c_2\\
        \vdots\\
        c_{2N-1}\\
        c_{2N}
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{align}
This property is ensured by $W$ being real and orthogonal, such that after the transformation the Majorana anticommutation relations are preserved. This can be shown using $b_\gamma = \sum_\alpha W_{\gamma \alpha} c_\alpha$, where $W_{\gamma \alpha}$ are just real numbers, meaning they commute
\begin{align}
    \{b_\gamma, b_\delta\} &= \sum_{\alpha, \beta} W_{\gamma \alpha} W_{\delta \beta} \{c_\alpha, c_\beta\} \\
    &= \sum_{\alpha, \beta} W_{\gamma \alpha} W_{\delta \beta} 2 \delta_{\alpha \beta} \\
    &= 2 \sum_{\alpha} W_{\gamma \alpha} W_{\delta \alpha}\\
    &= 2 \sum_\alpha W_{\gamma \alpha} W^T_{\alpha \delta} \\
    &= 2 \delta_{\gamma \delta} 
\end{align}
where the last step is a result of $W$ being orthogonal. The second property stems from the antisymmetry of $A$, such that in this basis $A$ acquires a canonical block-diagonal form
\begin{align}
    W A W^T = \begin{pmatrix}
        0 & \epsilon_1 & & & \\
        -\epsilon_1 & 0 & & &\\
        & & \ddots & & \\
        & & & 0 & \epsilon_N \\
        & & & - \epsilon_N & 0
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{align}
This condition stems from a mathematical theorem on the canonical form of real antisymmetric matrices, which states that if we have a real antisymmetric matrix $A$ of size $2N \times 2N$ there exists a real orthogonal matrix $W$ such that $A$ can be transformed into a block-diagonal form 
\begin{align}
    WAW^T = \text{diag}(C_1, C_2, \cdots, C_N), \hspace{4mm} C_j = \begin{pmatrix}
        0 & -\lambda_j \\
        \lambda_j & 0
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{align}
with $\lambda_j > 0$ being real numbers, stemming from the pairs of complex eigenvalues $\pm i \lambda_j$ for $A$. This theorem is a standard result in matrix analysis, and a proof can be found in \cite{zotero-item-34} \footnote{There are apparently many books on this topic, none of which i had access to. This is the best source i could find without proving it directly.}. Once we have a matrix $W$ that satisfies the above conditions we can reduce the Hamiltonian further to a canonical form by inverting the transformation $b_\gamma = \sum_\alpha W_{\gamma \alpha} c_\alpha$ to $c_\alpha = \sum_\gamma W_{\alpha \gamma}^T b_\gamma = \sum_\gamma W_{\gamma \alpha} b_\gamma$
\begin{align}
    H_\text{canonical} &= \frac{i}{4} \sum_{\alpha, \beta} A_{\alpha \beta} c_\alpha c_\beta\\
    &= \frac{i}{4} \sum_{\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta} A_{\alpha \beta} W_{\gamma \alpha} b_\gamma W_{\delta \beta} b_\delta \\
    &= \frac{i}{4} \sum_{\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta} W_{\gamma \alpha} A_{\alpha \beta} W_{\delta \beta} b_\gamma b_\delta\\
    &= \frac{i}{4} \sum_{\gamma, \delta} \left(W A W^T \right)_{\gamma \delta} b_\gamma b_\delta\\
    &= \frac{i}{4} \sum_{m=1}^N \begin{pmatrix}
        b_m' & b_m''
    \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}
        0 & \epsilon_m \\
        -\epsilon_m & 0
    \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}
        b_m'\\
        b_m''
    \end{pmatrix}\\
    &= \frac{i}{4} \sum_{m=1}^N \epsilon_m \left(b_m' b_m'' - b_m'' b_m' \right) \\
    &= \frac{i}{4} \sum_{m=1}^N \epsilon_m 2 b_m' b_m''\\
    &= \frac{i}{2} \sum_{m=1}^N \epsilon_m b_m' b_m'' = \sum_{m=1}^N \epsilon_m \left(\tilde{a}_m^\dagger \tilde{a}_m - \frac{1}{2} \right) \hspace{4mm} (\epsilon_m \geq 0).
\end{align}
Where we used that $\{b_m',b_m''\} = 0$ as they are separate components of $b$. In this form $b_m'$ and $b_m''$ are real linear combinations of $c_{2j-1}$ and $c_{2j}$, retaining the commutation relations. $N$ is the number of fermionic sites in the system, here $N = L$. The complex fermion quasiparticles are defined as $\tilde{a}_m = \frac{1}{2} \left( b_m' + i b_m'' \right) $ and $\tilde{a}_m^\dagger = \frac{1}{2} \left(b_m' - i b_m'' \right)$. 

The key take-away here is that the Kitaev chain Hamiltonian, due to being quadratic, can be diagonalized into independent quasi particles $\tilde{a}_m$ with energies $\epsilon_m$. This is how the problem can be reduced from a $2^L$-dimensional many-body problem to single-particle energies and modes on a $2L \times 2L$ matrix using BdG formalism as we will later cover \ref{sec:BdG}.

\subsection{Properties of Majorana fermion quasiparticles}
Let us take a minute to think about what a Majorana fermion quasiparticle really is. As the previous subsection also started by stating, a majorana fermion is a particle that is its own antiparticle. In other words, it obeys
\begin{align}
    c = c^\dagger. \label{eq:MF_Hermiticity}
\end{align}
At first glance\footnote{In fact, 6 months of first glances for some} it might appear to be a fairly tame statement. This is however a rather weird statement which gives the Majorana fermions interesting properties. This equation essentially enforces that applying a majorana fermion annihilation operator is equivalent to applying a creation operator: removing a majorana fermion is mathematically identical to creating one. 

This completely changes how we need to think about the Majorana fermion compared to a Dirac fermion. For Dirac fermions, when they are brought close, the Pauli principle sets in and keeps them apart. All combinations of Majorana raising and lowering operators however obey the anticommutation relation as seen in \ref{eq:Majorana_Herm_anticomm}, which does not enforce the Pauli exclusion principle for same states, it instead enforces $(c_l^\dagger)^2 = 1$. The anticommutation relation also however enforces $c_l^\dagger c_m^\dagger = - c_m^\dagger c_l^\dagger$ for $m \neq l$, implying the same exchange statistics for MFs as for Dirac Fermions. Furthermore, the number operator $n = c^\dagger_i c_i = c^2_i = 1$ does not make sense when looking exclusively at the MFs. Remember however that the Majorana fermions are quasi-particles which emerge from Fermionic sites $a_i$, and we have re-defined the sites using Majorana operators in (\ref{eq:define_dj}), which we called $d_i$. From this we can construct a meaningful number operator
\begin{align}
    n_i = d_j^\dagger d_j = \frac{1 + \gamma_1 \gamma_2}{2},
\end{align}
which has eigenvalues $n_i = 0, 1$. This number operator is experimentally measurable by bringing two MFs closer together and measuring the energy of the state. The energy will be caused by the overlap of the MFs and is described by the effective Hamiltonian
\begin{align}
    H_\text{eff} = \frac{i}{2}t c_{2i-1} c_{2i} = t \left(n_i - \frac{1}{2} \right),
\end{align}
which we will later discuss in more detail in \ref{subsec:_Overlap_Of_MFs}, where we also detail the strength of the overlap $t$. When two MFs are brought even closer and collide, (\ref{eq:MF_Hermiticity}) seems to imply that the MFs annihilate. We can however see that they instead pair up to form an ordinary Dirac fermionic site with an energy defined by this Hamiltonian.

Another property of MFs, perhaps obvious from the original derivation, is that they always come in pairs. A system has no trouble having an odd or even number of Dirac fermions, but there will always be an even number of MFs. The MFs can be separated in space, and so if a single MF appears somewhere in the system, another MF must appear somewhere else in the system. \cite{leijnseIntroductionTopologicalSuperconductivity2012} \cite{beenakkerSearchMajoranaFermions2013}

These are properties which typically arise from the particle-hole symmetry of superconducting systems, and is in fact an important reason as to why superconductors serve as a hunting ground for Majorana fermions. We will not be discussing the relevant properties of superconductors further \footnote{Although an attempt was made}. A good introduction to MFs in superconductors can be found in \cite{nayakNonAbelianAnyonsTopological2008}.

\section{Phases of the Kitaev chain} \label{sec:Phases_Kitaev_chain}
The Kitaev chain is really only interesting to us due to its promise of MZMs at its edges. We have previously seen that these quasiparticles only appear for specific parameters. In other words the Kitaev chain can host phases where the MZMs are, or are not present. Previously we have referred to these phases as the topologically non-trivial and the topologically trivial phase, respectively. The goal of this section is to further our understanding of which relation of parameters causes the chain to be in which phase, with a specific focus on the distinction between the topological and the trivial phase. 

\subsection{Phases of the bulk spectrum}
The bulk spectrum for energy vs. momentum $q$ for a chain of infinite length is given by 
\begin{align}
    \epsilon(q) = \pm \sqrt{\left(2 \omega \cos(q) + \mu\right)^2 + 4 |\Delta|^2 \sin^2(q)}, \hspace{4mm} - \pi \leq q \leq \pi. \label{eq:Bulk_spectrum}
\end{align}
Finding this relation is done by Fourier transforming the Hamiltonian to momentum space as shown in appendix \ref{Appendix:Hamiltonian_Transformation_momentum_space} and then finding its eigenvalues, as we will show in \ref{sec:BdG}. This was done using periodic boundary conditions as we expect the same result when $L \to \infty$ for a chain. For now you can take this for granted as its derivation gives little insight into the physics of the system. This energy spectrum will tell us where the system is gapped ($\epsilon(q) \neq 0$), and where gap closings ($\epsilon(q) = 0$) occur. The gap closings mark boundaries between topological phases \footnote{For a more detailed dive into how the closings of the bulk gap signify topological phase transitions see Sec. II.A in \cite{aliceaNewDirectionsPursuit2012}}. We now find the gap closings 
\begin{align}
    \epsilon(q) &= 0\\
    \pm \sqrt{\left(2 \omega \cos(q) + \mu\right)^2 + 4 |\Delta|^2 \sin^2(q)} &= 0 \\
    \left(2 \omega \cos(q) + \mu\right)^2 + 4 |\Delta|^2 \sin^2(q) &= 0
\end{align}
Both terms in this equation are real values squared, and must therefore be nonnegative. They must also both individually vanish to fulfill the equation, and we thus get two equations
\begin{align}
    2 \omega \cos(q) + \mu = 0, \hspace{4mm} \sin(q) = 0.
\end{align}
From the second equation we get $\sin(q) = 0$ for $q = 0, \pi$. Plugging this into the first equation we get
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    2 \omega \cos(0) + \mu &= 0\\
    2 \omega &= -\mu 
  \end{aligned}
  \qquad
  \begin{aligned}
    2 \omega \cos(\pi) + \mu &= 0 \\
      2 \omega &= \mu
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Meaning the bulk gap closes when $2|\omega| = |\mu|$, defining a phase boundary between the topologically trivial and non-trivial superconducting phases. 

From our previous findings we know that the system will be in the topologically non-trivial phase when $\mu < \omega$ (from the case $\omega = |\Delta| > 0, \mu = 0$), which is in the domain $|\mu| < 2|\omega|$. We here expect that if the parameters change without crossing the bulk gap boundary $2 |\omega| = |\mu|$ the system should remain in the same phase. Further we need $\Delta \neq 0$ to remain in the superconducting phase. Meaning the phases of the system can be divided into 
\begin{align}
    2|\omega| > |\mu|, \Delta \neq 0 &: \text{Topologically non-trivial superconductor (MZMs)}. \notag \\
    2|\omega| = |\mu|, \Delta \neq 0 &: \text{The superconducting bulk gap closes (phase boundary)}. \notag \\
    2|\omega| < |\mu|, \Delta \neq 0 &: \text{Topologically trivial superconductor (no MZMs)}.\notag \\
    2|\omega| > |\mu|, \Delta = 0 &: \text{Gapless metal}. \notag \\
    2|\omega| < |\mu|, \Delta = 0 &: \text{Gapped insulator}.\notag
\end{align}

\subsection{Boundary modes}
In the previous subsection we expected the phase of the system to remain the same as long as the bulk gap boundary is not crossed. We can check that this is true by studying the boundary modes, as they correspond to eigenvectors of $A$ localized near the ends of the chain, or in other words can confirm whether or not the phase is in the topological phase containing Majorana fermions. Such zero modes should be real linear combinations of $c_{2j-1}, c_{2j}$, having the form
\begin{align}
    b' = \sum_j \beta_j' c_{2j-1}, \hspace{4mm} b'' = \sum_j \beta_j'' c_{2j}
\end{align}
where $b'$ and $b''$ should be localized on opposite sides of the chain. Here $\beta_j', \beta_j''$ are real coefficients of the Majorana operators. If $b'$ and $b''$ are zero modes they have no energy and should commute with the Hamiltonian $\left[H, b'\right] = \left[H, b''\right] = 0$. Further, because the Hamiltonian only couples nearest neighbours the matrix $A$ will be tridiagonal with general values $a, b, c$, meaning the bulk (excluding the edge modes) can be expressed by
\begin{align}
    a' \beta_{j+1}' + b' \beta_j' + c' \beta_{j-1}' &= 0, \\
    a'' \beta_{j+1}'' + b'' \beta_j'' + c'' \beta_{j-1}'' &= 0.
\end{align}
The values $a, b, c$ for their respective $\beta$ will follow from the Hamiltonian; $b$ being the on-site term $\mu$, while $a$ and $c$ will be the off-diagonal pairing terms $(\omega \pm |\Delta|)$. These follow from the commutation relations mentioned above, and will allow us to find solutions for $b', b''$. We will here show the calculation for $b'$, and show the final result of $b''$, which has a similar calculation.
\begin{align}
    \left[H, b'\right] &= \sum_{l=1}^L \beta'_j \left[H, c_{2l-1}\right]\\
    &= \sum_{l=1}^L \beta'_j \left( i \mu \delta_{jl} c_{2j} + i (\omega + |\Delta|) \delta_{l, j+1} c_{2j} - i (- \omega + |\Delta|) \delta_{lj} c_{2j+2}\right)\\
    &= i \sum_{j=1}^L \mu \beta_j' c_{2j} + i \sum_{j=1}^{L-1} \beta_{j+1}' (\omega + |\Delta|) c_{2j} - \sum_{j=2}^{L-1} \beta_{j-1}' (-\omega + |\Delta|)c_{2j} \\
    \left[H, b'' \right] &= -i \sum_{j=1}^L \mu \beta_{j}'' c_{2j-1} - i \sum_{j=2}^{L-1} \beta_{j-1}'' (\omega + |\Delta|) c_{2j-1} + i \sum_{j=1} \beta_{j+1} (-\omega + |\Delta|) c_{2j-1}.
\end{align}
Where we used $\{c_m c_n, c_l\} = 2(\delta_{ln} c_m - \delta_{lm} c_n)$, crossed out terms where $\delta_{l+1, j}$ will always be 0, and redefined $j = j+1$ in the $c_{2j+2}$ term to make all terms $c_{2j}$. These terms are general. In the bulk, the three sums can be combined into one as we can ignore the boundary cases $j=1$ and $j = L$. And at the boundaries one of the three terms will be irrelevant (as we would have a missing site), but the statement $[H, b'] = [H, b''] = 0$ still holds true and gives the boundary conditions. Our problem now reduces to finding $\beta_j$ that are localized near a boundary and satisfy the above equations. Looking at the bulk we see that we can try solutions on the form $\beta_j' = x^j, \beta_j'' = x^{-j}$, and that the equations then will reduce to the same characteristic equation 
\begin{align}
    (\omega + |\Delta|)x^{j+1} + \mu x^j + (\omega - |\Delta|) x^{j-1} &= 0\\
    (\omega + |\Delta|)x^2 + \mu x + (\omega - |\Delta|) &= 0 \label{eq:characteristic_eq_x_boundaries}\\
    \to x_\pm = \frac{-\mu \pm \sqrt{\mu^2 - 4 \omega^2 + 4 |\Delta|^2}}{2(\omega + |\Delta|)}. \label{eq:x_pm_phases}\\
\end{align}
From this the general solutions follow on the form
\begin{align}
    \beta_j' &= \alpha_+' x_+^j + \alpha_-' x_-^j,\\
    \beta_j'' &= \alpha_+'' x_+^{-j} + \alpha_-'' x_-^{-j}.
\end{align}
With $\alpha_\pm$ being constants related to $x_\pm$. Therefore we can write an ansatz for the zero modes on either side of the chain
\begin{align}
    b' &= \sum_j \left(\alpha_+' x_+^j + \alpha_-' x_-^j \right)c_{2j-1} \\
    b'' &= \sum_j \left(\alpha_+'' x_+^{-j} + \alpha_-'' x_-^{-j} \right)c_{2j} 
\end{align}
We have previously confirmed that the topological phase boundary lies at $2|\omega| = |\mu|, \Delta \neq 0$, with expected majoranas at (a) $2|\omega| > |\mu|, \Delta \neq 0$, and no expected majoranas at (b) $2|\omega| < |\mu|, \Delta \neq 0$. We therefore want to check the boundary modes of these two phases to ensure that this is indeed correct, and we remain in the expected topological phase within these domains.

Before doing so we must clarify our expectations. If the zero mode $b'$ denotes a zero mode on the left side of the chain, then $b''$ will denote a zero mode on the right side (or vice versa). This is due to the exponential form $x^j$: If $|x_\pm| < 1$, then $x_\pm^j \to 0$ as $j$ increases, causing the zero mode $b'$ to be localized at the first site, $j = 1$. Whereas $x_\pm^{-j} \to \infty$ as $j$ increases, which after normalization causes $b''$ to be localized at the  end of the chain (again vice versa for $|x_\pm| > 1$). As such, we expect that when both $|x_\pm| < 1$ or $|x_\pm| > 1$ we have localized zero modes, with the second case causing them to have opposite orientations.

If however one solution is smaller than $1$, and the other is larger, $|x_\pm| < 1, |x_\mp| > 1$, we see that the zero modes $b', b''$ become a sum of one element approaching $0$, and another approaching $\infty$. In other words, one component is `localized' at an end, while the other is growing into the bulk. This is not a localized zero mode. If this were to produce a localized zero mode at one end of the chain, we'd need one of the $\alpha_\pm = 0$. This however breaks with boundary conditions. If we check the simplest missing state at the end of a chain $j=0$ for $b'$ we see that $\beta_0' = \alpha_+' + \alpha_-' = 0$, which is impossible to satisfy if one $\alpha_\pm = 0$, and the other $\alpha_\mp \neq 0$. Similar arguments can be made for both ends of the chain for both $b', b''$. As such, we expect the case $|x_\pm| < 1, |x_\mp| > 1$ to produce no localized zero modes.

Figuring out when $|x_\pm|$ is larger than or smaller than $1$ is not directly obvious, but it can be done with a mathematical trick. If we cast $|x_\pm|$ into the complex plane and setting $|x_\pm| = 1 = e^{i\theta}$ to be the unit sphere, we can figure out the conditions on the parameters when $|x_\pm|$ crosses the unit sphere. Plugging this into our characteristic equation \ref{eq:characteristic_eq_x_boundaries} we find 
\begin{align}
    \left(\omega + |\Delta|\right) e^{i\theta} + \mu + \left(\omega - |\Delta| \right) e^{-i\theta} &= 0 \\
    2\omega \cos(\theta) + 2 i |\Delta| \sin(\theta) + \mu &= 0
\end{align}
Where the complex and the real terms have to equal $0$ separately, meaning we require $\sin(\theta) = 0 \to \theta = 0, \pi$, giving the solution
\begin{align}
    2\omega &= \pm \mu\\
    2 |\omega| &= |\mu|.
\end{align}
Meaning that the value of $|x_\pm|$ can only cross $1$ when this condition is met\footnote{It is perhaps unsurprising and definitely reassuring that this is the same result as earlier}. As such, we can study the domains above and below this condition to determine which topological phase the domain belongs to, and we will remain in this phase if this boundary is not crossed.

\subsubsection{(a) $2|\omega| < |\mu|, \Delta \neq 0$}
From our condition above we see that we can safely consider the case $\omega = |\Delta|$, which significantly simplifies the problem
\begin{align}
    x_\pm &= \frac{-\mu \pm \sqrt{\mu^2 - 4 \omega^2 + 4 \omega^2}}{4 \omega}\\
    &= \frac{-\mu \pm \mu }{4\omega}\\
    &\to x_- = \frac{-\mu}{2\omega}, \hspace{4mm} x_+ = 0\\
    &\to |x_-| < 1, \hspace{4mm} |x_+| > 1.
\end{align}
If we were to use different signs for the variables the inequalities would simple be reversed, but never the same for both $|x_\pm|$. As seen from our discussion above this corresponds to the zero modes not existing in this case. 

\subsubsection{(b) $2\omega > |\mu|, \Delta \neq 0$}
If we again consider $\omega = |\Delta|$ we find
\begin{align}
    x_\pm &= \frac{-\mu \pm \sqrt{\mu^2 - 4 \omega^2 + 4 \omega^2}}{4 \omega}\\
    &= \frac{-\mu \pm \mu }{4\omega}\\
    &\to x_- = \frac{-\mu}{2\omega}, \hspace{4mm} x_- = \frac{0}{2\omega}\\
    &\to |x_-| < 1, \hspace{4mm} |x_+| < 1.
\end{align}
In this case we have Majorana zero modes $b'$ localized near $j=0$, and $b''$ near $j = L$. And if we change the sign convenction to $-\omega > |\mu|$, we simply flip the position of $b'$ and $b''$. This confirms our initial expectation that the zero modes will remain in the system when the parameters are changed, as long as $|\Delta| \neq 0$ and we do not cross the boundary from the domain $2|\omega| > |\mu|$ to $2|\omega| < |\mu|$.

\subsection{Overlap of the Majorana wave functions} \label{subsec:_Overlap_Of_MFs}
This subsection might have been better placed in the previous section on Majorana fermions. It is however dependent on the previous discussion on boundary modes, and is therefore placed here for a more pedagogical flow. 

Up until this point, the discussion has really been for either a periodic chain, or a chain with $L \to \infty$. But in a realistic system, we'd have a finite $L$. Recalling the discussion above, the localized MZMs are actually not actually perfectly localized, but rather the coefficients of the majorana operators decay exponentially fast into the bulk in the topological phase. So for a system with finite $L$ there would be some overlap between the MZMs which would decrease exponentially with the length of the chain. This overlap would cause some coupling between the MZMs, and we would have a non-zero energy based on the overlap. Seeing as $j$ is real and $|x_\pm| < 1$, we can describe the tail of the coefficients in the bulk as 
\begin{align}
    |x|^j &= e^{j \ln|x|} \\
    &= e^{-j |\ln|x||} \\
    &= e^{-j l_0}.
\end{align}
This will be governed by whichever root decays the most slowly, so we define $l_0^{-1} = \text{min}(|\ln|x_-||, |\ln|x_+||)$. The tail of the leftmost MZM would go as $e^{-j |ln|x_\pm||}$ whereas the tail of the rightmost MZM would go as $e^{-(L-j) |ln|x_\mp||}$. Multiplying them together we find the overlap $t \propto e^{-L/l_0}$. Using this, we can describe the interaction by an effective Hamiltonian on the form 
\begin{align}
    H_\text{eff} &= \frac{i}{4} \sum_{l, m} A_{lm} b_l b_m\\
    &= \frac{i}{4}\left(t b' b'' - t b'' b' \right)\\
    &= \frac{i}{4}\left(t b' b'' + t b' b'' \right)\\
    &= \frac{i}{2} t b' b'' \\
    &= t (d^\dagger d - \frac{1}{2}).
\end{align}
This will cause an energy splitting between the ground states, and they will be split by an energy $t$. A high $t$ would break the degeneracy of the ground states, causing great problems for our topological protection and quantum computation schemes as a whole. In fact, $t$ can be interpreted as the tunneling amplitude from one edge to the other for the fermionic quasiparticles, offering the quasiparticles a weak form of communication, which breaks their topological protection for high $t$. As such, figuring out which values of $t$ are necessary to for the system to be in a degenerate ground state, maintaining topological protection and allowing quantum computation is an important aspect of the field. The most obvious way to maintain a low $t$ is simply by making the chain $L$ longer, separating the MZMs as much as possible. Another way of lowering $t$ is by making the tail $|x|$ decay faster, which could be done by minimizing $|x|$ in \ref{eq:x_pm_phases} for all relevant sites with respect to the parameters $\mu, \omega, \Delta$ for both the trivial and the non-trivial phase. 

\section{BdG} \label{sec:BdG}
BdG formalism can offer great reductions in the dimensions of the problem, and is as such a common formalism throughout the field of superconductivity as it is relevant for quadratic Hamiltonians. The method takes a quadratic Hamiltonian and casts it into a diagonal form, resembling a system of non-interacting particles \cite{bonarettiBogoliubovValatinTransformationFermionic2026}. In our case it takes the exponentially growing Hilbert space $(2^N \times 2^N)$, and reduces it to growing linearly as $2N \times 2N$. This could provide an enormous decrease in numerical cost of simulating the system.

In this section we show the general BdG formalism. First we discuss the time-independent case and $t = 0$, diagonalizing it and finding the ground and excited states. Then we explore a method of time-evolving the operators while remaining in the BdG formalism. This largely follows \cite{mascotManyBodyMajoranaBraiding2023a}. Then we apply the time-independent case to the Kitaev chain Hamiltonian, producing results directly relevant to the discussion above. We do not apply the time-evolution to the Kitaev chain Hamiltonian here, instead we do this in \ref{sec:Time_dependent_Kitaev_BdG} for pedagogical reasons.

Starting from the general (time-dependent) Hamiltonian in the BdG form
\begin{align}
    H(t) &= \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix}
        a^\dagger  & a
    \end{pmatrix} \mathcal{H}_\text{BdG}(t)\begin{pmatrix}
        a\\
        a^\dagger
    \end{pmatrix} \label{eq:General_H_BdG}\\
    \mathcal{H}_\text{BdG}(t) &= \begin{pmatrix}
        \mathcal{H}_{ij}(t) & \Delta_{ij}(t)\\
        \Delta_{ji}^*(t) & -\mathcal{H}_{ij}^*(t)
    \end{pmatrix}. \label{eq:General_BdG_Form_H}
\end{align}

\subsection{Diagonalizing $\boldsymbol{H(0)}$ by a Bogoliubov transformation}
We wish to perform a Bogoliubov transformation in order to diagonalize the Hamiltonian at $t = 0$. We introduce $d_n, d_n^\dagger$ by
\begin{align}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        a_i\\
        a_i^\dagger 
    \end{pmatrix} &= \sum_n \begin{pmatrix}
        U_{in} & V_{in}^*\\
        V_{in} & U_{in}^*
    \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}
        d_n \\
        d_n^\dagger
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{align}
We can equivalently write this without the indices, showing that this is simply an expansion of the original fermion operators $a$ into new fermion operators $d$ by
\begin{align}
    a & = U d + V^* d^\dagger \label{eq:bogoliubov_trans_a}\\
    a^\dagger &= V d + U^* d^\dagger \label{eq:bogoliubov_trans_a_dag}.
\end{align}
And we can define the transformation matrix as
\begin{align}
    W = \begin{pmatrix}
        U & V^*\\
        V & U^*
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Conditions on the Bogoliubov transformation}
Following the anticommutation relations of fermion operators, this poses conditions on $U$ and $V$. We find them by studying the anticommutators
\begin{align}
    \left\{a_i, a_j^\dagger \right\} &= \sum_{nm} \left\{U_{in} d_n + V_{jm}^* d_m^\dagger, V_{jn} d_n + U_{jn}^* d_n^\dagger \right\}\\
    &= \sum_{nm} \left[ U_{in} V_{jm} \left\{d_n, d_m\right\} + U_{in} U_{jm}^* \left\{d_n, d_m^\dagger \right\} + V_{in}^* V_{jm} \left\{d_n^\dagger, d_m \right\} + V_{in}^* U_{jm}^* \left\{d_n^\dagger d_m^\dagger \right\}\right] \notag \\
    &= \sum_n \left[ 0 + U_{in} U_{jn}^* + V_{in}^* V_{jn} + 0\right]\\
    &= \sum_n \left[ U_{in} \left(U_{jn}^*\right)^T + V_{in}^* V_{jn}^T\right] = \delta_{ij}\\
    &\to U U^\dagger + V^* V^T = I.
\end{align}
Where we used the fermionic anticommutation relations in addition to 
\begin{align}
    \left\{a A, b B\right\} &= ab\left\{A,B\right\}\\
    \left\{A + B, C + D\right\} &= \left\{A, C\right\} + \left\{A, D\right\} + \left\{B, C\right\} + \left\{B, D\right\}
\end{align}
where $a$ and $b$ are numbers, and $A, B, C, D$ are operators. We can similarly find
\begin{align}
    \left\{a_i, a_j \right\} &= \sum_{nm} \left[ U_{in} U_{jm}\left\{ d_n, d_m \right\} + U_{in} V_{jm}^* \left\{d_n, d_m^\dagger \right\} + V_{in}^\dagger U_{jm} \left\{ d_n^\dagger, d_m\right\} + V_{in}^* V_{jm}^* \left\{d_n^\dagger, d_m^\dagger \right\}\right] \notag \\
    &= \sum_n \left[0 + U_{in}V_jn + V_{in}^* U_{in} + 0 \right] = 0\\
    &\to UV^\dagger + V^* U^T = 0.
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Substituting the transformation into $\boldsymbol{H(0)}$}
Choosing non-negative energies $E_n$ we can define the diagonal BdG Hamiltonian to be
\begin{align}
    W^\dagger \mathcal{H}(0) W &= \begin{pmatrix}
        E & 0 \\
        0 & -E
    \end{pmatrix},
\end{align}
where the $-E$ stems for the particle-hole symmetry, apparent from the general form \ref{eq:General_BdG_Form_H}. We now substitue \ref{eq:bogoliubov_trans_a} \ref{eq:bogoliubov_trans_a_dag} into the general Hamiltonian \ref{eq:General_H_BdG}
\begin{align}
    H(0)&= \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix}
        d^\dagger & d
    \end{pmatrix} W^\dagger \mathcal{H}(0) W \begin{pmatrix}
        d \\
        d^\dagger
    \end{pmatrix}\\
    &= \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix}
        d^\dagger & d
    \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}
        E & 0 \\
        0 & -E
    \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}
        d\\
        d^\dagger
    \end{pmatrix}\\
    &= \frac{1}{2} \sum_n E_n \left( d^\dagger_n d_m - d_n d^\dagger_n \right)\\
    &= \sum_n E_n \left(d_n^\dagger d_n - \frac{1}{2} \right) \label{eq:BdG_Energy}.
\end{align}
Where we again used the anticommuation relations of $d_n$.

\subsection{Finding the ground state of the diagonalized H(0)}
From the energy \ref{eq:BdG_Energy} we can tell that the ground state is the vacuum of all particles $|\boldsymbol{0}_d\rangle$, where
\begin{align}
    d_n |\boldsymbol{0}_d\rangle = 0, \hspace{4mm} \text{for all $n$}.
\end{align}
This is called the Bogoliubov vacua. There are several ways of finding this state, we explore the Thouless and the product state.

\subsection{BdG form of the Kitaev chain Hamiltonian}
We want to express the Hamiltonian in momentum space as
\begin{align}
    H = \frac{1}{2} \sum_q \Psi_q^\dagger \mathcal{H}_\text{BdG} \Psi_q,
\end{align}
using the Nambu basis (spinors)
\begin{align}
    \Psi_q^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix}
        a_q^\dagger & a_{-q} 
    \end{pmatrix}, \hspace{4mm} \Psi_q = \begin{pmatrix}
        a_q \\
        a_{-q}^\dagger 
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{align}
Where the momentum-space fermioninic annihilation and creation operators $a_q, a_q^\dagger$ are defined by the fourier transformation 
\begin{align}
 a_j = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_q e^{iqj}a_q, \hspace{4mm} a_j^\dagger = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_q e^{-iqj}a_q^\dagger.
\end{align}
Where $q = \frac{2 \pi n}{N}$ with $n$ such that $q$ runs over the Brillouin zone $[-\pi, \pi]$. We see that $\mathcal{H}_\text{BdG}$ has to be a $2 \times 2$ matrix. If we look at an arbitrary $\mathcal{H}_\text{BdG}$ we can see that after multiplication with the Nambu spinors the Hamiltonian becomes
\begin{align}
    H &= \frac{1}{2} \sum_q \begin{pmatrix}
        a_q^\dagger & a_{-q} 
    \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}
        a(q) & b(q)\\
        c(q) & d(q)
    \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}
        a_q \\
        a_{-q}^\dagger 
    \end{pmatrix} \\
    &= \frac{1}{2} \sum_q \left( a(q) a_q^\dagger a_q  + b(q) a_q^\dagger a_{-q}^\dagger + c(q) a_{-q} a_q + d(q) a_{-q} a_{-q}^\dagger\right).
\end{align}
This should match the momentum-space transformed Hamiltonian as found in \ref{Appendix:Hamiltonian_Transformation_momentum_space}. We therefore rewrite the Hamiltonian to match the above description. We use the anti-commutation relations of the operators together with $q = -q$ from the periodic boundary conditions, and drop inessential constants 
\begin{align}
    H &= \sum_q \left[-\mu a_q^\dagger a_q - 2 \omega \cos(q) a_q^\dagger a_q  - i \sin(q) \left( \Delta a_q a_{-q} + \Delta^* a_q^\dagger a_{-q}^\dagger\right) \right] \\
    &= \frac{1}{2} \sum_q \left[ 2\left(- \mu - 2 \omega \cos(q) \right) a_q^\dagger a_q  - 2 i \sin(q) \left( \Delta a_q a_{-q} + \Delta^* a_q^\dagger a_{-q}^\dagger\right) \right]\\
    &= \frac{1}{2} \sum_q \left[ \left(- \mu - 2 \omega \cos(q) \right) a_q^\dagger a_q + \left(- \mu - 2 \omega \cos(q) \right) a_q^\dagger a_q  - 2 i \sin(q) \left( \Delta a_q a_{-q} + \Delta^* a_q^\dagger a_{-q}^\dagger\right) \right] \\
    &= \frac{1}{2} \sum_q \left[ \left(- \mu - 2 \omega \cos(q) \right) a_q^\dagger a_q + \left(\mu + 2 \omega \cos(-q) \right) a_{-q} a_{-q}^\dagger - 2 i \sin(q) \left( \Delta a_q a_{-q} + \Delta^* a_q^\dagger a_{-q}^\dagger\right) \right].
\end{align}
We see that we must have
\begin{align}
    a(q) &= -\mu -2 \omega \cos(q)\\
    b(q) &= - 2 i \Delta \sin(q) \\
    c(q) &= 2 i \Delta^* \sin(q) \\
    d(q) &= \mu + 2\omega \cos(-q) = \mu + 2\omega \cos(q)\\
    \to \mathcal{H}_\text{BdG} &= \begin{pmatrix}
        -\mu -2 \omega \cos(q) & - 2 i \Delta \sin(q) \\
        2 i \Delta^* \sin(q) & \mu + 2\omega \cos(q)
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{align}
