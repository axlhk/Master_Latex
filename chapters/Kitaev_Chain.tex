\chapter{The Kitaev chain}
The Kitaev chain is a toy model of a one dimensional spinless p-wave superconductor, and is an example of material that can realize a topological superconducting phase \cite{kitaevUnpairedMajoranaFermions2001}. It consists of a chain of spinless fermions with nearest-neighbour hopping and superconducting pairing, under the influence of a chemical potential. The Hamiltonian takes the form 
\begin{align}
    H =& \sum_j\left[ - \omega \left(a_j^\dagger a_{j+1} + a_{j+1}^\dagger a_j  \right) - \mu \left(a_j^\dagger  a_j - \frac{1}{2} \right) + \Delta a_j a_{j+1} + \Delta^* a_{j+1}^\dagger a_j^\dagger \right]
    \label{eq:Original_Kitaev_Hamiltonian}
\end{align}
where $a_j$ and $a_j^\dagger$ are annihilation and creation operators for the fermionic sites $j$ in the chain. $\omega$ is a hopping amplitude, $\mu$ is the chemical potential, and $\Delta = |\Delta|e^{i\theta}$ is the induced superconducting gap. The Hamiltonian serves to give rise to Majorana fermion quasiparticles at its boundaries. These Majorana fermions can be used as qubits, and a network of chains can be used for quantum computation. 

Majorana fermions are interesting candidates for qubits as their topological nature provides robustness against local perturbations and certain sources of decoherence. Their non-Abelian nature also allows for braiding, where the majorana fermions exchange position and information about the path is stored in the wavefunction. 

\section{Majorana fermions}
A Majorana fermion is a particle that is its own antiparticle. The Majorana operators can be defined as
\begin{align}
    c_{2j-1} = a_j + a_j^\dagger, \hspace{4mm}  c_{2j} = \frac{a_j - a_j^\dagger}{i}, \hspace{4mm} (j = 1, ..., N) \label{eq:majorana_op2j}
\end{align}
which are hermitian, and anticommute, meaning they satisfy the relations
\begin{align}
    c_m^\dagger = c_m, \hspace{4mm} c_l c_m + c_m c_l = 2 \delta_{lm}, \hspace{4mm} (l,m = 1, ..., 2N). \label{eq:Majorana_Herm_anticomm}
\end{align}
Using the definitions of the majorana operators \ref{eq:majorana_op2j} we can find the number operator for a site $j$
\begin{align}
    a_j^\dagger a_j = \frac{1}{2} \left(1 + i c_{2j-1} c_{2j} \right),
\end{align}
where the term $i c_{2j-1} c_{2j}$ corresponds to a phase error. If $c_{2j-1}$ and $c_{2j}$ are not on the same site, but separated in space, then $c_{2j-1} c_{2j}$ would represent an interaction between two separated Majorana sites, requiring the environment to couple the Majorana fermions simultaneously to create the phase error. Physical noise and imperfections are typically local, so placing the Majorana sites far enough away from each other would decrease the risk of a phase error. Further, a single Majorana operator cannot appear by itself in a reasonable Hamiltonian as it would not preserve fermionic parity; flipping the system from an even to odd (or vice versa) number of fermions. As such, the Majorana operators will always come in pairs $c_i c_l$, and thus no parity preserving term can involve just one isolated Majorana. Together this tells us that errors would either have to break parity, or couple both separated Majoranas at once, which is highly unlikely and thus the Majorana fermions are protected from local errors.

\subsection{Rewriting the Hamiltonian in terms of Majorana operators}
We can express the Hamiltonian in terms of Majorana operators. As we will see, this transformation will provide insight about the structure of the phases and the emergence of the edge modes. Before doing so we simplify the calculation by hiding the phase $\theta$ in the majorana operators
\begin{align}
    c_{2j-1} &= e^{i \frac{\theta}{2}} a_j + e^{-i \frac{\theta}{2}}a_j^\dagger \label{eq:c2j-1_hide_phase}\\
    c_{2j} &= -ie^{i \frac{\theta}{2}} a_j + ie^{-i \frac{\theta}{2}}a_j^\dagger \label{eq:c2j_hide_phase}.
\end{align}
We can now perform the transformation of the Hamiltonian by inverting these relations and inserting into the original Hamiltonian \ref{eq:Original_Kitaev_Hamiltonian}. Together with the hermiticity and anticommutation relations \ref{eq:Majorana_Herm_anticomm} we get the Hamiltonian
\begin{align}
    H &= \frac{i}{2} \sum_j \left[-\mu c_{2j-1} c_{2j} + \left(\omega + \left|\Delta \right|  \right) c_{2j} c_{2j+1} + \left(-\omega + \left| \Delta \right| \right) c_{2j-1} c_{2j+2} \right].
\end{align}
This calculation is shown in appendix \ref{Appendix:Hamiltonian_Transformation}. Staring at this Hamiltonian, we can first see that it is on the general quadratic Majorana form
\begin{align}
    H = \frac{i}{4} \sum_{l, m} A_{lm} c_{l} c_m
\end{align}
with A being a real and antisymmetric matrix, encoding the intrinsic particle-hole symmetry of the superconducting system. The single particle spectrum is fully determined by the eigenvalues of $A$, reducing the problem from a $2^{L}$-dimensional many-body operator problem in Fock space to a single-particle linear algebra problem on a $2L \times 2L$ matrix. This is the basis of BdG, as we will get back to later. Further, we see that all direct mentions of the complex phase $\theta$ are gone from $H$. This reflects that $\theta$ can be removed by an appropriate gauge transformation, and is therefore irrelevant for the bulk spectrum and the topological classification of the phases, which we will also touch upon later.

The Majorana representation also gives us a geometric picture of the chain. The Hamiltonian now consists of three terms, providing three different types of couplings. An on-site term $-\mu c_{2j-1} c_{2j}$, and two nearest-neighbour terms $(\omega + |\Delta|)c_{2j} c_{2j+1}$ and $(-\omega + |\Delta|) c_{2j-1} c_{2j+2}$. The on-site term couples two Majorana modes per site $j$, so for a chain of length $L$ we get $2L$ Majorana operators. We can garner further insight by studying specific cases for the parameters $\mu, \omega$ and $|\Delta|$ of the Hamiltonian.

\subsubsection{Trivial case \texorpdfstring{$\boldsymbol{\omega = |\Delta| = 0, \mu > 0}$} {$\omega = |\Delta| = 0, \mu > 0$}}
In the case where $\mu$ dominates the Hamiltonian reduces to
\begin{align}
    H &= -\mu \sum_j \left(a_j^\dagger a_j - \frac{1}{2} \right) = \frac{i}{2} \mu \sum_j c_{2j-1}c_{2j}.
\end{align}
In this case the system is topologically trivial as the Majoranas on each site are glued together to form a complex fermion. There are no unpaired Majorana fermions at the edges of the chain.

\subsubsection{Topological case $\boldsymbol{\omega} = \boldsymbol{|\Delta|} > 0, \boldsymbol{\mu} = 0$}
In the case where the hopping and superconducting parameters dominate the Hamiltonian reduces to
\begin{align}
  H = i\sum_{j} \omega c_{2j} c_{2j+1}, \label{eq:H_tdelta_mu0}  
\end{align}
where the nearest neighbours between different sites dominate. This is an interesting case as $c_2$ couples to $c_3$, $c_4$ couples to $c_5$, and so on. But $c_1$ and $c_{2L}$, the Majorana edge states, do not appear in the Hamiltonian. They have however not disappeared physically, but rather remain as zero-energy Majorana modes localized at the edges of the wire. Together they form a nonlocal fermionic zero mode. This is the most important insight from the transformation of the Hamiltonian as these are the states we were looking to create.

We can now define new annihilation and creation operators spanning the sites $j$ and $j+1$
\begin{align}
    d_j &= \frac{1}{2} \left(c_{2j} + i c_{2j+1} \right) \\
    d_j^\dagger &= \frac{1}{2} \left(c_{2j} - i c_{2j+1} \right).
\end{align}
Such that we can rewrite the Hamiltonian 
\begin{align}
    H &= 2 \omega \sum_{j = 1} \left(d_j^\dagger d_j - \frac{1}{2} \right).
\end{align}
As the edge states are not mentioned in the Hamiltonian, this expression fully determines the energy spectrum of the bulk. We see that the bulk consists of fermionic modes with energy $\propto 2\omega$. Further, the ground states satisfy the condition $d_j |\psi \rangle = 0 $ for all $j$. As such, all these modes will remain empty, and any ground state $|\psi \rangle $ is fully determined by the edge majoranas $c_1$ and $c_{2L}$. Therefore we get two orthogonal states where the above ground state condition for the bulk is true; $|\psi_0\rangle$ and $|\psi_1 \rangle$. These ground states will be eigenstates of the hermitian operator $-i c_{1} c_{2L}$ as the edge Majoranas represent unpaired degrees of freedom. This operator also squares to 1, and as such has eigenvalues $\pm 1$. We get 
\begin{align}
    - i c_{1} c_{2L} |\psi_0 \rangle = |\psi_0\rangle, \hspace{4mm} - i c_{1} c_{2L} |\psi_1 \rangle = -|\psi_1\rangle,
\end{align}
where the labeling of the states were chosen arbitrarily. We see that the total fermion parity operator
\begin{align}
    P = \prod_j \left(- i c_{2j-1} c_{2j} \right)
\end{align}
has the same contribution from the bulk for both ground states. So the difference in parity is solely a result of the edge contribution $-i c_1 c_{2L}$. The even parity of $|\psi_0 \rangle$ represents the state being a superposition of states with an even number of electrons. And $|\psi_1 \rangle$ has an odd parity, representing an odd number of electrons. Together they reflect the boundary degrees of freedom from the unpaired Majorana modes at the edges.
